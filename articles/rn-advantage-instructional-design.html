The RN Advantage: Why Clinical Experience Makes Better Instructional Designers

By Becky Olamide, RN, PhD, MPH Published: March 8, 2025 7 min read ✓ Clinically Reviewed by Author

When a nursing school or healthcare organization hires an instructional designer, they usually look for someone who knows ADDIE, can build in Articulate, and has a portfolio of eLearning modules. Those are fair requirements. But there is a deeper question most hiring managers never ask: does this person actually understand what happens at the bedside?

That gap matters more than most people realize. Clinical accuracy in nursing education is not just about getting the facts right. It is about understanding which facts matter most when a patient is deteriorating, which assessment finding changes everything, and which piece of content needs to hit harder because a nurse who misses it will make a dangerous error. You cannot design that kind of learning experience from a textbook.

I have worked as a registered nurse in ICU, L&D, OB, PACU, and Postpartum. I hold a PhD in Psychology and a Master of Public Health. I also design nursing curricula and healthcare eLearning for schools, hospitals, and publishers. That combination is not common, and it changes everything about how I approach a course.

What Non-Clinical Designers Miss

A 2026 study published in Nurse Educator by researchers at Texas A&M found that as nursing education shifts toward online, hybrid, and simulation-based formats, course design has grown significantly more complex. The study noted that faculty, though content experts, often lack training in instructional design and digital pedagogy. The inverse is equally true and less discussed: instructional designers often lack the clinical depth to make good decisions about healthcare content.

This shows up in specific, predictable ways. A non-clinical designer building a post-operative monitoring module might correctly list the vital sign parameters to watch. What they miss is the sequencing of concern, the clinical gestalt that tells an experienced nurse something is wrong before any single number crosses a threshold. That pattern recognition only comes from time at the bedside, and it is exactly what simulation scenarios and branching eLearning need to capture.

Clinical accuracy in nursing education is not just about getting the facts right. It is about knowing which facts carry the most weight when a patient's condition is changing and a nurse has 90 seconds to decide.

Research from NCSBN's Fall 2025 publication on nursing education confirms that clinical education remains an underdeveloped aspect of nursing curriculum, with educators sometimes emphasizing task completion over the cognitive and affective domains that build genuine clinical judgment. An instructional designer who has never functioned as a clinician is poorly positioned to correct that gap. They will often replicate it.

What Clinical Experience Actually Contributes

Content Prioritization

Not all clinical information is equal, and a nurse knows which content needs the most instructional weight. In a hemodynamics module, the difference between a MAP of 65 and 60 is not just a number. It is the line between adequate perfusion and end-organ damage. A clinician knows to build that distinction into a scenario with real consequence. A non-clinical designer may treat it as a data point in a table.

Scenario Realism

Simulation scenarios and branching eLearning live or die on clinical realism. When a scenario feels fake, nurses disengage immediately. They have seen real patients. They know what a deteriorating post-op patient looks like, sounds like, and what the chart shows before the situation becomes critical. Building scenarios that hold up under that scrutiny requires firsthand clinical experience, not research alone.

I developed 16 mid-fidelity manikin-based simulation scenarios for a nursing skills lab. Every branching decision point in those scenarios came from real clinical patterns I had seen in PACU and ICU. The client's feedback was that the scenarios felt like actual patient encounters. That is not something you can produce from a literature review.

Assessment Design

Writing clinical questions that actually assess judgment rather than memorization requires clinical experience. The Next Generation NCLEX moved away from single-answer recall questions precisely because nursing boards recognized that memorization does not predict safe practice. Writing NGN-aligned items, including Bowtie questions, Matrix questions, and unfolding case studies, requires someone who can think through a clinical situation from the inside, not just describe it from the outside.

The Collaboration Model vs. the Integrated Model

Some organizations solve this by pairing a non-clinical instructional designer with a clinical subject matter expert. The SME provides content, the designer builds the course. This model works reasonably well when the relationship is structured and both parties have time. In practice, it often breaks down.

SMEs are almost always overextended. They are practicing clinicians or full-time educators with limited bandwidth for extended content review cycles. When the collaboration is rushed, clinical review becomes shallow, and errors slip through. The designer, lacking clinical context, cannot identify what the SME missed.

The integrated model, where one person holds both the clinical expertise and the instructional design skills, removes that friction. Review cycles are faster. Content decisions are better informed. The person building the course understands both the learning science and the clinical stakes.

This is not an argument against collaboration. It is an argument for knowing what you are buying when you hire an instructional designer for healthcare content. A designer with genuine clinical experience is a fundamentally different product, not a premium version of the same thing.

What to Look For When Hiring

If you are a nursing school, hospital education department, or healthcare publisher evaluating instructional designers, here are the questions that reveal clinical depth:

  • Can they describe a specific clinical error they have caught in content they were hired to review?
  • Do their simulation scenarios include realistic clinical timelines, not just checklists?
  • Can they explain why a particular assessment finding matters clinically, not just identify it?
  • Have they written questions aligned to the NCJMM, and can they explain how each step maps to clinical decision-making?
  • Do they understand the difference between PACU, stepdown, and ICU acuity, and can they design content that reflects those differences?

A designer who can answer those questions from experience is not just more qualified. They are protecting your learners from content that sounds right but teaches the wrong thing.

Key Takeaway

Clinical experience is not a bonus credential for an instructional designer working in healthcare. It is the difference between content that is technically accurate and content that actually prepares nurses for what they will face in practice. When you hire for healthcare curriculum design, the bedside experience behind the portfolio matters as much as the portfolio itself.
