What Every Healthcare Content Writer Should Know About HIPAA

By Becky Olamide, RN, PhD, MPH Published: April 7, 2026 7 min read ✓ Clinically Reviewed by Author

Most healthcare content writers know HIPAA exists. Fewer understand which parts of it apply to their work specifically, how the Business Associate Agreement works, and what the 2025 and 2026 regulatory updates mean for anyone producing content that touches Protected Health Information.

This is not an abstract compliance concern. The Office for Civil Rights levied more than $6.6 million in HIPAA fines in 2025 alone, targeting organizations ranging from small practices to large health systems. A freelance healthcare writer who works with case study material, patient testimonials, or clinical documentation without understanding their HIPAA obligations is taking a risk that is easily avoided with the right baseline knowledge.

I am a registered nurse who handled Protected Health Information daily across ICU, PACU, L&D, and OB settings. I developed scenario-based HIPAA compliance training during my clinical career and now produce healthcare content under contracts that include Business Associate Agreements. This article covers the specific HIPAA concepts that matter most for healthcare content writers in 2026.

The Four Rules Content Writers Need to Understand

The Privacy Rule

Governs who can use and disclose Protected Health Information (PHI). For content writers, this is most relevant when working with patient case studies, testimonials, or any identifiable patient data. PHI includes names, dates, locations, medical record numbers, and any information that could identify a patient.

The Security Rule

Governs electronic PHI (ePHI). If a healthcare client shares patient data with you electronically, how you store, transmit, and protect that data falls under Security Rule requirements. This applies even if you are a freelancer receiving files by email.

The Breach Notification Rule

Requires covered entities and business associates to notify affected individuals and HHS within specific timeframes if PHI is breached. If you are a business associate and your systems are compromised, notification obligations apply to you.

The Omnibus Rule

Extended HIPAA requirements to business associates directly, not just through their covered entity clients. This means freelance content writers who qualify as business associates are directly liable for HIPAA compliance, not just their clients.

Are You a Business Associate?

This is the first question every healthcare content writer needs to answer honestly. A Business Associate is a person or organization that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a Covered Entity (a healthcare provider, health plan, or healthcare clearinghouse) in the course of providing a service.

If a hospital hires you to write patient education materials and gives you access to patient demographic data to personalize the content, you are likely functioning as a Business Associate. If a medical practice asks you to write case studies using de-identified patient scenarios they have prepared, you may not be. The distinction matters because Business Associates must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with their covered entity clients, and are directly liable for HIPAA compliance under the Omnibus Rule.

If you are unsure whether a project puts you in Business Associate territory, the safest approach is to ask your client whether a BAA is required before the project begins. A client who cannot answer that question is itself a compliance risk signal.

A freelance healthcare writer who receives identified patient data, handles clinical documentation, or produces content from patient records is functioning as a Business Associate whether or not they have signed a BAA. HIPAA obligations attach to the function, not the contract title.

What Protected Health Information Actually Means for Content Work

PHI is any individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity or its business associate. The 18 HIPAA identifiers include names, dates (including birth dates, admission dates, and discharge dates), geographic data below state level, telephone numbers, email addresses, Social Security numbers, medical record numbers, account numbers, and any other unique identifying number or code.

For content writers, this has specific practical implications:

  • Case studies using real patient information require either patient authorization or de-identification that meets the Safe Harbor or Expert Determination standard. A case study that omits a patient's name but includes their age, specific diagnosis, hospital unit, and admission date may still be identifiable.
  • Patient testimonials for healthcare organizations require written HIPAA authorization from the patient before use, separate from any marketing consent form.
  • Clinical vignettes for training content that are drawn from real patient encounters need to be de-identified or composited to the point where no reasonable person could identify the individual.
  • Ghostwritten physician content that references specific patient encounters requires careful review of what identifiable details are included.

The 2025 and 2026 Updates That Affect Content Work

2026 Update
The HIPAA Privacy Rule update finalized in April 2024 prohibits the use or disclosure of PHI to investigate or penalize individuals for obtaining or providing lawful reproductive healthcare services. For content writers producing case studies, clinical documentation, or training content that involves reproductive health, this creates new constraints on how that information can be shared and used. Full compliance with Notice of Privacy Practices updates was required by February 16, 2026.
Pending Rule
The proposed HIPAA Security Rule update, with a final rule targeted for May 2026, would make encryption and multi-factor authentication mandatory for all covered entities and business associates handling ePHI, eliminating the "addressable" flexibility organizations have historically relied on. Freelancers who receive ePHI from clients should be implementing encryption for stored files and MFA for accounts that access that data now, regardless of whether the final rule has been published.

The reproductive health privacy update is particularly relevant for writers working in women's health, obstetrics, family planning, and related fields. Content that involves identifiable patient information related to reproductive care now carries heightened restrictions on how it can be used, shared, or disclosed, and the June 2025 federal court ruling that vacated part of the original rule created additional uncertainty that writers working in this space should discuss explicitly with their clients and their clients' legal counsel.

Practical Steps for Healthcare Content Writers

Before Starting Any Healthcare Project

  • Ask your client directly whether the project requires you to function as a Business Associate and whether a BAA is needed.
  • Clarify whether any patient-identifiable information will be shared with you, and if so, how it will be transmitted and stored.
  • Confirm the de-identification standard for any patient information included in your deliverables.
  • Review any patient authorization or consent forms that the client says cover the content use you are supporting.

During the Project

  • Store any PHI in encrypted files and use secure file transfer when sending deliverables that contain patient information.
  • Do not retain PHI longer than necessary to complete the contracted work.
  • Do not use patient information for any purpose beyond what is authorized in the contract and any applicable patient authorization.
  • If you discover a potential breach (lost device, unauthorized access, inadvertent disclosure), notify your client immediately. Your BAA will specify the timeframe and process.

Building HIPAA Competency as a Career Asset

Healthcare organizations, hospitals, and publishers who hire content writers and instructional designers for clinical content increasingly evaluate HIPAA literacy as part of their vendor assessment. A writer who can demonstrate hands-on HIPAA experience, has worked under BAAs, and understands the current regulatory landscape is a lower-risk contractor than one who has to be educated from scratch on every project.

My HIPAA experience is hands-on: I handled PHI daily as a bedside nurse, documented under HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules, and have developed scenario-based HIPAA compliance training. That background is not something most content writers bring to a healthcare contract, and it matters to clients who have compliance obligations they need their vendors to understand.

The Bottom Line for Freelancers

HIPAA applies to freelance healthcare writers when they function as Business Associates, which happens when they create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI in the course of their work. The obligations that attach are real and are directly enforced under the Omnibus Rule. The regulatory environment in 2026, with active OCR enforcement, pending Security Rule updates, and the new reproductive health privacy protections, makes baseline HIPAA literacy more important for healthcare content professionals than it has ever been.

The good news is that HIPAA compliance for a freelance content writer is not complex. It requires knowing which projects trigger Business Associate obligations, signing appropriate BAAs, handling PHI with basic security practices, and staying current on regulatory updates that affect your work. None of that is beyond reach, and all of it protects both you and the clients who are trusting you with their most sensitive patient information.

Key Takeaway

Healthcare content writers who receive, handle, or produce content from Protected Health Information are likely functioning as Business Associates under HIPAA, with direct compliance obligations. The 2025 and 2026 regulatory updates, including the reproductive health privacy rule and the pending Security Rule changes, make this a particularly active compliance moment. Understanding your obligations before a project starts is far less costly than discovering them after something goes wrong.
