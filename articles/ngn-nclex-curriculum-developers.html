NGN Is Here: What the New NCLEX Format Means for Curriculum Developers

By Becky Olamide, RN, PhD, MPH Published: May 8, 2025 8 min read ✓ Clinically Reviewed by Author

The Next Generation NCLEX launched on April 1, 2023. Two years in, many nursing schools have updated their test banks and added NGN-style questions to their exams. Far fewer have done what actually matters: restructured their curriculum to build the clinical judgment that NGN is designed to measure.

Those two things are not the same. Adding NGN question formats to existing content is a surface fix. The exam changed because NCSBN research showed that newly licensed nurses were increasingly expected to make complex decisions under pressure, and the old NCLEX was not reliably measuring whether they could. If your curriculum still teaches students to recognize and recall rather than recognize, analyze, and act, the question format is the least of your problems.

This article is for curriculum developers, nursing faculty, and instructional designers building or revising nursing content in 2025. It covers what NGN actually requires, where most programs are still falling short, and what genuine curriculum alignment looks like in practice.

What Changed and Why It Matters

The NGN is built around the NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (NCJMM), a six-step framework that mirrors how nurses actually think through patient care situations in practice:

  1. Recognize Cues - identify relevant information from a clinical scenario
  2. Analyze Cues - determine what the information means clinically
  3. Prioritize Hypotheses - rank the most likely and most urgent explanations
  4. Generate Solutions - identify appropriate nursing actions
  5. Take Action - select and implement the right intervention
  6. Evaluate Outcomes - assess whether the intervention worked

The old NCLEX could measure steps 4 and 5 reasonably well with multiple choice questions. It struggled with 1, 2, 3, and 6. NGN closes that gap with new item types that require students to work through a clinical situation across all six steps, often within a single case study.

Pass rate data from Nurse.org shows the transition clearly. From 2022 to 2023, first-time RN pass rates jumped from 79.9% to 88.56%, the sharpest single-year swing in recent NCLEX history. By 2025, rates had settled to 88.6% through mid-year. The programs that moved early to embed clinical judgment into their curriculum had a head start. The ones that waited showed up in the data.

The New Item Types Curriculum Must Prepare Students For

Item Type What It Measures Curriculum Implication
Bowtie Condition, actions to take, parameters to monitor simultaneously Students need practice linking assessment findings to interventions and monitoring in one integrated step
Matrix/Grid Multiple actions or findings across multiple patients or conditions Curriculum must build comparative clinical reasoning, not just single-patient recall
Highlight Text Identify clinically significant information within a chart or note Students need exposure to realistic EHR-style documentation, not just structured questions
Drop-Down Cloze Complete a clinical statement by selecting the correct option from a dropdown Requires precise clinical language and context-sensitive reasoning
Extended Drag-and-Drop Sequence or categorize clinical actions Prioritization must be explicitly taught, not assumed from content exposure
Unfolding Case Study Six sequential questions across one evolving patient scenario The most demanding item type. Requires curriculum built around case-based, sequential clinical reasoning

Adding NGN question formats to existing content is a surface fix. If your curriculum still teaches recognition and recall rather than analysis and clinical judgment, the question format is the least of your problems.

Where Most Curriculum Falls Short

Teaching Facts Instead of Reasoning

Most nursing curriculum was built to teach content domains: cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, pharmacological. Students learn what conditions look like, what the normal values are, what the standard interventions are. That knowledge is necessary but not sufficient for NGN.

NGN scenarios present a patient with multiple concurrent findings across several body systems and ask the student to reason through what is most urgent, most likely, and most actionable right now. That kind of reasoning has to be practiced, not just introduced. Curriculum that covers content domains without building the connective tissue between them leaves students well-informed but poorly prepared for how the exam actually works.

Assessments That Don't Match the Exam

Research on NGN question writing published in Nursing Education Perspectives found that faculty need a solid understanding of the NCJMM framework and psychometric principles to write items that genuinely measure clinical judgment rather than content recall dressed up in a new format. A Bowtie question that can be answered correctly through memorization is not measuring what it is supposed to measure. It is just a harder multiple choice question.

Poorly constructed NGN-style items are worse than well-constructed traditional items because they give programs false confidence that their students are prepared when they are not. The format looks right. The measurement does not.

Simulation Not Aligned to NCJMM

Simulation is one of the most powerful tools for building clinical judgment, but only when it is explicitly designed around the six NCJMM steps. A simulation scenario that walks students through a clinical situation without requiring them to articulate their reasoning at each decision point is a missed opportunity. Debriefing that focuses on what happened rather than how students thought through the situation builds technical skill without building judgment.

What Genuine NGN Alignment Looks Like

Curriculum that genuinely aligns to NGN does several specific things:

  • Uses case-based learning throughout, not just in simulation or capstone courses. Students should be reasoning through patient scenarios from the first semester, building complexity as they progress.
  • Maps learning objectives explicitly to NCJMM steps. Not every objective covers all six steps, but every objective should be traceable to at least one.
  • Includes EHR-style documentation in learning activities. Highlight Text items require students to identify clinically significant information in realistic chart formats. If students have never read a real progress note or MAR, they are unprepared for this item type.
  • Builds prioritization explicitly. The single most common weakness in NCLEX preparation is prioritization, and NGN measures it directly. Curriculum needs to require students to justify their priorities, not just identify the right answer.
  • Writes assessments at the application and analysis level of Bloom's taxonomy minimum. Knowledge-level items do not prepare students for NGN. Every knowledge-level item in a course assessment is a missed opportunity to build judgment.

I have written 150-item balanced NCLEX blueprints that include all NGN item types, unfolding case studies mapped to all six NCJMM steps, and standalone clinical judgment items across all eight Client Needs subcategories. The difference between a blueprint that looks NGN-aligned and one that actually is comes down to whether the clinical reasoning at every decision point reflects real bedside thinking, not textbook thinking.

Key Takeaway

NGN is not a formatting problem. It is a curriculum problem. Programs that added new question types to old content structures are one preparation cycle away from discovering the gap. The schools that are performing well in 2025 built clinical judgment into their curriculum before students ever saw a Bowtie question. That sequence matters, and it is not too late to get it right.
