How to Build a Freelance Career as a Nurse Writer and Instructional Designer

By Becky Olamide, RN, PhD, MPH Published: February 10, 2026 8 min read ✓ Reviewed by Author

Most freelance writers can write. Most instructional designers can build a course. What almost none of them can do is walk out of an ICU shift and accurately describe what it feels like when a post-operative patient's hemodynamics start trending in the wrong direction, or write a simulation scenario that experienced nurses find clinically credible, or catch the cardiovascular physiology error in an eLearning module before it reaches learners.

That is the asset nurses bring to freelance healthcare writing and instructional design. Clinical experience that took years to build is not something a competitor can replicate by taking an online course. The freelance market for nurses who write and design is real, growing, and significantly underserved by people who combine genuine bedside experience with strong writing and instructional design skills.

I am an ICU and PACU nurse with 10 years of bedside experience, a PhD in Psychology, and a Master of Public Health. I am a Top Rated Plus freelancer on Upwork with 97 completed jobs, 937+ hours billed, and a 95% job success score. This is what I know about building this kind of career from the ground up.

The Market Is Real and the Demand Is Growing

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects that employment of training and development specialists will grow 11% from 2024 to 2034, much faster than average for all occupations. Healthcare is one of the highest-demand sectors for that work. Nursing schools, hospitals, healthcare publishers, eLearning companies, pharmaceutical firms, health technology companies, and medical device manufacturers all need clinical content they can trust. Most of them have experienced firsthand what happens when they hire a non-clinical writer or designer to produce it.

For nurse writers specifically, the demand spans patient education materials, continuing education content, clinical training modules, nursing curriculum, medical blog content, ghostwritten articles for healthcare executives, and academic support work for nursing students and researchers. A nurse who can do more than one of these things competently has a range that most competitors cannot match.

Three to five polished writing samples are enough to open doors at the early career stage. Relevance matters more than volume. Clients want to see that you can write for different audiences and formats, not that you have accumulated a large number of bylines.

Start With What You Already Have

The most common mistake nurses make when entering freelance writing or instructional design is undervaluing what they already know and overestimating what they still need to learn. You do not need a writing degree to write well about clinical topics you understand deeply. You do not need an instructional design certification to build a credible course outline based on how you actually learned clinical skills.

What you need first is evidence that you can do the work. That means samples. If you have no published work, write two or three on-spec pieces that demonstrate your clinical knowledge and your ability to communicate it clearly to a target audience. A well-written 800-word patient education piece on post-operative monitoring, a case study format exploring a clinical scenario, or a concise explainer on a nursing education topic you know well are all usable portfolio pieces that can be produced in a week without a client contract.

For instructional design specifically, a sample course outline with stated learning objectives, a short storyboard for a module section, or a set of NCLEX-style questions with rationales demonstrates the core skills before you have a client to demonstrate them for. These do not need to be polished final products. They need to be good enough to show you can think like an instructional designer.

Building the Career in Phases

Phase 1

Build two to three strong samples and set up a professional presence

A simple portfolio page with your clinical background, your target services, and your samples is enough to start. LinkedIn is the highest-return platform for healthcare freelancers: a strong profile that leads with your clinical credentials and lists your freelance services specifically attracts clients who are already looking for what you offer. Your RN license, clinical specialties, and any graduate credentials belong in the headline, not buried in the experience section.

Phase 2

Start pitching directly and set a rate that reflects clinical expertise

The most effective early clients for nurse writers are small medtech companies, health-tech startups, nursing education companies, and independent healthcare publishers. These organizations need clinical content, often lack an in-house clinical reviewer, and respond well to a direct pitch that leads with clinical credentials. Set your rate based on your expertise, not your experience as a writer. A nurse with 10 years of ICU experience writing about critical care monitoring is not a beginner writer. They are a clinical expert who also writes. Those are different products at different price points.

Phase 3

Build a platform presence that does the pitching for you

Upwork is the most effective platform for healthcare freelance writers and instructional designers who want inbound clients rather than spending all their time pitching. A strong Upwork profile with completed jobs, client reviews, and a clear positioning statement attracts clients who are already filtering for clinical expertise. The Top Rated and Top Rated Plus badges signal reliability and track record to clients who are evaluating multiple candidates. Getting there requires completed projects with strong reviews, which means the first few jobs need to be priced to win and delivered exceptionally.

Phase 4

Specialize and expand your service range

The most sustainable freelance healthcare careers are built around a clear clinical niche combined with a range of service types. A critical care nurse who writes medical content, designs CEU modules, and supports nursing curriculum development is competing in multiple markets with one unified credential set. That breadth, anchored to a clinical specialty that gives it credibility, is harder to replicate and commands higher rates than either writing or instructional design alone.

What Separates Sustainable Careers From Short-Term Gigs

Client Relationships Over Transactions

The freelancers who build durable healthcare careers are the ones who turn one-project clients into ongoing relationships. Healthcare organizations that find a writer or designer they trust with clinical content do not go looking for a replacement. They come back, they refer colleagues, and they expand the scope of work over time. Delivering exceptional work on the first project is the most effective business development strategy available to a freelance nurse writer.

Documented Clinical Expertise

Your resume and your Upwork profile tell clients what you have done. Your portfolio tells them how you think. Case studies that describe a clinical problem, the instructional solution, and the outcome are more persuasive than a list of deliverables. A potential client reading that you designed 16 simulation scenarios for a nursing skills lab and that the client reported they felt like real clinical encounters is reading something a non-clinical competitor cannot offer. Document your work at that level of specificity.

Continuing to Develop

The healthcare and eLearning fields both move fast. Nurses who stay current on changes to NCLEX formats, NGN item writing, INACSL simulation standards, and AI developments in healthcare content are more valuable than those who are not. Conference attendance, even virtually, professional reading, and ongoing clinical currency all compound over time into a differentiated expertise that the market consistently rewards.

The Honest Part

Freelancing is not passive income. It requires consistent client development, reliable delivery, and the discipline to run a small business while also doing the clinical and instructional work. In the early stage, the income is inconsistent and the hours are long because you are building a client base while simultaneously developing your craft and your business systems.

What makes it worthwhile for nurses specifically is that the asset you are monetizing is something you built over years of clinical work that you were going to do anyway. Your ICU experience, your clinical judgment, your ability to catch an error that a non-clinical writer would miss, your credibility with the nursing educators and healthcare organizations that are your clients: these are not things you have to build from scratch. You built them at the bedside. The freelance career is what you do with them next.

Key Takeaway

The freelance healthcare writing and instructional design market rewards clinical expertise that most competitors cannot replicate. Nurses who combine genuine bedside experience with strong writing or design skills and build a portfolio that documents both are competing in a market with very few people who can do what they do. The career takes time to build, but the asset it is built on took years of clinical work to develop. That asset does not depreciate. It compounds.
