AI-Generated Medical Content vs. Human-Written: What's the Real Risk?

By Becky Olamide, RN, PhD, MPH Published: December 1, 2025 8 min read ✓ Clinically Reviewed by Author

The question most healthcare organizations are asking is whether AI can write their clinical content. The question they should be asking is what happens when it gets it wrong.

AI-generated medical content is now everywhere: patient education materials, clinical training modules, CEU content, nursing curriculum, healthcare blogs, and medical documentation. The speed and cost advantages are real. A large language model can produce a draft in minutes that would take a human writer hours. For organizations under pressure to produce more content faster, the temptation to hand that work to AI is understandable.

The problem is that AI hallucinates. In general contexts, a hallucination means the model produces confident, fluent, plausible-sounding content that is factually wrong. In medical contexts, that same mechanism produces confident, fluent, plausible-sounding clinical information that could mislead a patient, misinform a learner, or contribute to a dangerous clinical decision. The distinction between plausible and accurate matters everywhere. In healthcare, it matters most.

These numbers come from peer-reviewed research published in 2025. The 31% hallucination rate in AI-generated clinical notes comes from a study published in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence evaluating LLM-generated ambient scribes against physician-authored notes. Notably, the AI notes were rated as more thorough and better organized than physician notes. They were also more prone to hallucination: fabricated clinical details, symptoms the patient never reported, and information that sounded right but was not.

The physician portal message study, published in npj Digital Medicine, is particularly revealing. Twenty practicing primary care physicians reviewed AI-generated draft responses to patient messages. Four of the 18 messages contained errors: objective inaccuracies or potentially harmful omissions. Each error was insufficiently addressed by 13 to 15 of the 20 physicians. Between 35% and 45% of the erroneous drafts were submitted completely unedited. Eighty percent of the physicians reported that the AI drafts reduced their cognitive workload. Seventy-five percent found them safe.

That combination is the core risk: AI-generated medical content reduces the cognitive effort of review while simultaneously making errors harder to catch. A physician who feels less mentally taxed is a physician who reviews less carefully.

Why Medical AI Errors Are Harder to Catch Than They Look

AI hallucinations in general writing are often obvious: a fabricated quote, a wrong date, an invented statistic. In medical writing, hallucinations are much harder to identify because they are contextualized. The model has enough medical training data to place an incorrect clinical detail in a setting where it is syntactically and contextually plausible. A wrong drug dosage surrounded by accurate clinical information looks like accurate information. A mischaracterized physiological mechanism embedded in a well-structured explanation reads as correct explanation.

This is what ECRI, the patient safety organization, was identifying when they named insufficient AI governance as the number two patient safety threat for 2025. Their concern was not that AI is being used in clinical settings. It is that the speed of adoption has outpaced the development of validation frameworks, oversight structures, and clinician training on how to review AI output critically.

AI models are only as good as the algorithms they use and the data on which they are trained. In clinical settings, staff may misinterpret AI-generated results if they are not familiar with the limitations of AI capabilities. And as AI becomes more subtly infused into digital health, it may be difficult to track when AI is at fault for errors. — ECRI Patient Safety Report, 2025

Columbia University researchers, including Dr. Maxim Topaz who keynoted the 2025 NLN Education Summit, published a commentary in npj Digital Medicine in September 2025 specifically on AI scribes in clinical practice. Their conclusion was direct: the speed of adoption has outpaced validation, transparency, and regulatory oversight. They also flagged a specific equity concern: speech recognition systems used by AI scribes are less accurate when transcribing Black patients' speech compared to white patients, meaning errors are not randomly distributed across the patient population.

The Specific Risks in Healthcare Content Writing

Clinical Education and Curriculum

When AI-generated content reaches nursing curricula, eLearning modules, or clinical training materials, the hallucination risk compounds. A clinical error in a patient portal message reaches one patient. A clinical error in a nursing education module reaches every student who completes it. The error propagates at scale before anyone catches it, particularly if the organization lacks a clinical SME review process that goes beyond formatting and grammar checks.

I caught a cardiovascular physiology error in AI-assisted content during a clinical review engagement. The error involved a hemodynamic concept presented correctly in isolation but misapplied in a clinical scenario. It had passed an initial review because the surrounding content was accurate and the error was stated confidently. It took someone with actual ICU experience to recognize that the clinical decision tree being taught would produce the wrong response in a real patient situation.

Patient Education Materials

AI-generated patient education content presents a different risk profile. Patients are not clinical reviewers. They are reading content to make decisions about their own health, and they have no framework for identifying a plausible-sounding clinical error. A hallucinated medication interaction, an inaccurate symptom description, or a misrepresented treatment timeline in a patient education handout can directly affect health behavior and care-seeking decisions.

Academic and Research Writing

The 13.5% figure for AI-generated biomedical abstracts represents a different but related problem. Research published in PubMed in July 2026 documented that AI in medical writing produces both hallucinations in clinical descriptions and fabricated or inaccurate citation lists. A fabricated reference in a clinical education context means the evidence base for a clinical claim cannot be verified, because the source the AI cited does not exist or does not say what the AI claims it says.

What Responsible AI Use in Medical Writing Looks Like

The answer is not to avoid AI in healthcare content development. It is to use it with an understanding of what it cannot do and to build review processes that account for its failure modes.

  • Use AI for structure and drafting, not for clinical authority. AI handles outline generation, first drafts, content organization, and boilerplate language well. Clinical claims, dosing information, physiological mechanisms, and anything that directly affects patient care decisions need human clinical review, not just editorial review.
  • Treat AI output as a starting point, not a deliverable. The efficiency gain from AI-assisted drafting is only realized if the review process is genuinely substantive. Reviewing AI output for tone and formatting while missing clinical errors is worse than not using AI at all, because it creates false confidence that the content has been reviewed.
  • Use clinicians for clinical review, not editors. An experienced editor can catch grammar errors, improve clarity, and improve structure. They cannot reliably catch a mischaracterized hemodynamic mechanism or a wrong drug interaction. Clinical review of clinical content requires clinical expertise.
  • Verify references independently. AI-generated citations require individual verification. The reference may not exist, may not say what the AI claims, or may be accurately cited but not actually support the clinical claim it is attached to.
  • Document AI use and review processes. For any clinical content used in healthcare settings, accreditation or regulatory requirements may eventually require disclosure of AI use and evidence of clinical review. Building that documentation practice now is less costly than retrofitting it later.

The Human Element That Cannot Be Automated

The fundamental limitation of AI in medical writing is the same limitation that makes clinical judgment irreplaceable at the bedside. AI generates output based on patterns in training data. It does not know which patterns matter most in a specific clinical context, which edge cases invalidate the general rule, or which patient population the content is actually written for.

A nurse who has monitored hemodynamics in an ICU does not just know that a MAP below 65 is concerning. They know what that number looks like when it is trending down slowly over four hours in a post-cardiac surgery patient who just received a diuretic, and they know why that presentation demands a different response than the textbook cutoff suggests. That contextual clinical knowledge is what separates accurate medical writing from plausible medical writing. AI cannot generate it. It can only pattern-match it, and pattern-matching fails exactly when it matters most.

Key Takeaway

AI-generated medical content hallucinates at measurable rates, produces errors that are harder to catch than obvious mistakes, and reduces the cognitive engagement of reviewers who might otherwise catch those errors. The risk is not that AI is being used in healthcare writing. It is that it is being used without the clinical oversight that clinical content requires. Plausible is not accurate, and in healthcare, the difference between the two is what patient safety depends on.
